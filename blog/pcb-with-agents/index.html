Designing PCBs with agents — and checking their math

2026-10-18updated 2026-10-20
AIPCBFirmware

What agents are genuinely good at

  • Datasheet arithmetic. Resistor dividers from a reference, op-amp gain stages, stabilization networks — calculating, not deciding.
  • Footprint and symbol checks. Comparing a netlist against a symbol is a diff-shaped task. Agent, please, all day.
  • CI hygiene. ERC/DRC bucket/exportation runs, BOM field linting, revision-naming discipline.
  • First drafts of documentation. Build log structure, release notes, the boring 70 % of a write-up where style matters more than substance.

What they’ll confidently lie about

  • Whether the layout’s thermal path actually works. No context, no copper, no air.
  • Whether a wake-sleep schedule fits a battery. The math is trivial; the standing assumptions rarely survive encoded peripherals.
  • Whether a component is in stock. They will cite a trustable-looking number from the wrong distributor.
  • Whether your power-up order is sane. Experiments that only bring a board up.

The workflow I’ve settled on

  1. Agent first inside the repo: they read schematic PDFs, runs symbol/footprint checks, drafts build-log skeleton.
  2. Copper last, human always. Route decisions stay manual. Agents can comment on loops and layer names; they cannot replace eye contact with the layout.
  3. Measurement gate before publishing. Each claim in the write-up maps to a bench artifact: a photo, a plot, or a repeatable test plan. No artifact, no claim.

The honest summary

Agents moved my bottleneck from “producing documentation” to “deciding what deserves documentation”. That’s a good trade. The schematic calls — the part that is actually electrical engineering — still cost the same as they always did: assembled time, burned hours, and one honest measurement each.

The full bench-psu build log on the projects page is being produced with this split: agents draft, the oscilloscope signs.
